Aim and prediction

Compare <A> & <B>

Apparatus and principle

Two matched virtual dissolution cells hold equal added salt masses and equal water preparations. Grain count changes the initial edge and exposed area. Dissolution transfers salt into the solution while conserving total salt. Capacity is0.36g/gwater at25°C; selected solid density2.17g/cm³. The assumed flux acts on the changing area and remaining saturation drive. Numerical integration uses RK4 with0.05s mesh; physical model time is separate from presentation time.

Procedure and recording instructions

  1. Write the aim: compare equal added salt masses split into different numbers of grains. Predict which will dissolve more quickly and whether the final capacity will change.
  2. Record water mass, added solid mass, initial saturation fraction, both grain counts, chosen flux and model time. Both cells share all preparation conditions except the grain count.
  3. Record the initial edges and exposed areas before starting. Compare one grain with64 equal grains: for equal mass the smaller edges give more total exposed area. The fully wetted, separated-grain assumption is part of this model.
  4. Run the guide and pause at several model times. Record remaining solid, additional dissolved salt, current area, capacity and rates for both cells. The moving100-second guide interval represents180 model seconds; use the model clock.
  5. Apply capacity=0.36×water mass and a₀=cbrt(m₀/(ρN)). Calculate area=6Na², driving fraction=max(0,1−d_total/capacity), and rate=flux×area×driving fraction. Use the numerical edge-loss result for the current shrinking shape; do not substitute initial area as if it never changed.
  6. Repeat with equal or reversed grain counts, pre-saturated water, excess solid and zero flux. Separate the equilibrium limit from the amount actually dissolved by the selected time. A faster start does not guarantee a greater rate after the finer sample is depleted.
  7. Check dissolved plus remaining salt against the original salt account. Include the initially dissolved salt in the total; it is not newly dissolved from the added grains. Write the result, a student conclusion, and the model limitations.

Precautions and limitations

  • The chosen flux is a teaching parameter, not a measured sodium-chloride kinetic coefficient. Surface-area and saturation feedback are modeled at fixed25°C; stirring, heat transfer and real diffusion-layer changes are not predicted.
  • Capacity uses a rounded reference0.36g salt per g water. The selected approximate solid density is2.17g/cm³. These constants do not establish experimental precision or official curriculum certification.
  • Equal cubes represent separated, fully wetted grains with all six faces exposed. Agglomeration, contact shielding, fragmentation, crystal defects, gravity and settling are omitted. Fixed positions are a schematic comparison, not a force-equilibrium prediction.
  • Water density1g/mL and apparent dissolved-salt volume0.28mL/g are chosen drawing approximations. The mixture envelope includes embedded solid material; it must not be counted as another full volume of pure solution.
  • The shrinking edge is computed by RK4 integration with a0.05-second mesh and a final fractional step. A stable edge-loss variable retains very small transfers near saturation. Floating-point and integration errors remain; a numerically zero residual rate is not a measured equilibration time.
  • Only additional dissolution counts as conversion of the added grains. Initially dissolved salt is included separately. Equal grain mass and solvent capacity remain matched; breaking grains changes exposed area rather than increasing the available capacity.
  • Mean rate is unavailable at the initial instant. Area and initial-rate ratios are unavailable when their denominator vanishes. An initially saturated or zero-flux control retains the added grains; zero flux alone does not imply thermodynamic equilibrium.
  • Reported mass excludes apparatus tare. Computational digits, model-derived observations and a virtual guide are not physical measurements. Physical experiments require suitable teacher supervision; this is a virtual investigation.
