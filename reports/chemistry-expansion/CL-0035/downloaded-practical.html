Compare melting and dissolving

Science Atlas 360° · VisionariesAI Labs Private Limited · Class 3 · CL-0035

Aim

Distinguish pure ice changing phase from salt forming a solution, using mass, temperature and heat evidence from separate vessels.

Apparatus and principle

Two model vessels with equal starting sample masses: pure ice initially at 0 °C on a temperature-held 25 °C heating base, and sodium chloride in a separate 25 °C water sample. During equilibrium melting, supplied latent heat changes phase while the sample remains at 0 °C; after melting the water warms. Dissolving disperses salt ions in a solvent without producing molten salt. Both processes conserve their separate matter budgets.

Prediction

Ice needs heat to change into liquid water. Salt can dissolve in separate water without becoming molten salt. Stirring should change the chosen rate, while less solvent can leave excess salt. Both vessels should conserve their own matter.

Procedure

  1. Prepare 10 g ice and 10 g salt in separate vessels; the salt vessel contains 100 g water. Set heat conductance 0.3 W/K and the stirred condition.
  2. Record zero time, pause during melting, then pause just before and after the predicted full-melt time. Record ice, melted water, sample temperature and supplied heat.
  3. Record undissolved salt, dissolved salt and solution mass at the same time. Salt ions are invisible at apparatus scale.
  4. Check ice + melted water = initial ice mass. Separately check solvent + dissolved salt + undissolved salt = total matter in the right vessel.
  5. Use fresh matched trials: change only heat conductance to zero; compare still and stirred solution conditions; reduce solvent to 20 g while keeping the same initial salt mass. Record at matched times.
  6. Apply latent-heat, liquid-warming, chosen dissolution-rate and concentration substitutions. Write a result supported by measurements, a conclusion and one model limitation.

Result and conclusion

Melting changed ice into liquid water while conserving the 10 g ice-side budget. Its sample stayed at 0 °C during coexistence, then warmed after the predicted 444.66667 s completion. Salt dispersed into separate water and still contributed to solution mass. At 360 s, the chosen stirred condition dissolved more salt than still water. With only 20 g water, capacity left excess salt. These are maintained-temperature teaching models with chosen conductance/rates; solution volume and resolved heat or fluid flow were not measured.

Precautions and limitations

Do not mix salt with the ice: these are separate processes. Initially ice is already at its melting point. Bath and solution temperatures are maintained; vessel heat capacity, evaporation, solution enthalpy and resolved heat/fluid flow are absent. Constant conductance and exponential salt-transfer rates are chosen, not calibrated experimental measurements. A shrinking cube and 60 model grains encode solid mass, not measured shapes or grain counts. Reference densities are approximate. The left meltwater geometry accounts for ice displacement and eventual flotation; the right liquid fill is schematic because solution volume is not solved. Stirrer motion illustrates the condition without claiming a computed vortex. Concentration denominators for solvent and solution differ. Guide transfer time is 11.25×, manual time 1×.
