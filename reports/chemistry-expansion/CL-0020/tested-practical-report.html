Make droplets join on a smooth surface

Science Atlas 360° · VisionariesAI Labs · CL-0020 · Class 2

Aim

Observe contact and joining, conserve water volume, and compare initial and eventual liquid–air surface areas.

Apparatus and principle

Two magnified tiny water drops on an ideal smooth plane, controlled approach and timer. A 90° contact angle is assumed; starting and final drops are hemispheres. Surface tension favours lower liquid–air area at conserved volume in this idealized setting.

Prediction

The two water volumes should add when the drops touch. The final joined drop should have less curved surface area than the two starting drops. If they remain separated, two drops should remain.

Procedure

  1. Choose 1 µL for each drop and 1 mm initial gap.
  2. Record before contact, then run the controlled approach and relaxation.
  3. Record the joined state and compare total volume and curved surface area.
  4. Repeat with unequal volumes.
  5. Turn off controlled contact and repeat: do the drops cross the gap?
  6. Write a conclusion and explain why the chosen geometric timings cannot predict real coalescence speed.

Result and conclusion

Two 1 µL drops join to a represented volume of 2 µL. Their curved liquid–air area decreases from 7.676633 to 6.092948 mm², about 20.63%. A no-contact trial retains two drops. The model uses an ideal 90° contact angle and a volume-conserving geometric relaxation; its chosen timings do not predict real coalescence speed.

Precautions and limitations

The view is magnified. Approach is externally controlled. At chosen contact time 8 s, two tangent caps are replaced by one volume-conserving half-spheroid; microscopic bridge growth is not resolved. A chosen geometric relaxation reaches a hemisphere at 16 s. Gravity, contact-line pinning, viscosity, evaporation, contamination and variable contact angle are excluded. Reference surface tension approximates water near 20 °C. This is not a fluid solver or calibrated coalescence-time prediction. At ideal contact angle 90°, replacing dry solid with wet solid has zero net interfacial-energy contribution in the ideal Young relation; reported energy concerns the curved liquid–air interface. Endpoint pressure calculations apply to hemispheres, not the stretched intermediate. Keep the no-contact result separate from hypothetical joined-state calculations. Internal checks do not imply independent expert or official board certification.
