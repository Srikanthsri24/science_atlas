Compare floating and sinking everyday objects

Science Atlas 360° · VisionariesAI Labs · CL-0008

Aim

Compare four ideal solid spheres of equal volume and different chosen densities in still water.

Apparatus and principle

Transparent tank, 150 mm water depth, four 20 cm³ sphere models, force indicators and timer. Weight acts downwards. Buoyancy depends on submerged volume. Water drag opposes motion. A bottom contact supplies support.

Prediction

For equal solid-sphere volumes, wood and the chosen polymer should float; glass and steel should sink.

Procedure

  1. Keep volume, release height and water unchanged.
  2. Select a sphere, release it from rest and observe entry into the water.
  3. Pause at an early time and record the transient forces.
  4. Record at 12 seconds; floating spheres may still bob slightly.
  5. Compare all four spheres, using density and force evidence.

Result and conclusion

At equal 20 cm³ volume, the lower-density wood and polymer models float, while glass and steel sink. At 12 s the floating cases still bob, so their transient buoyancy need not equal weight exactly. Bottom support balances weight minus buoyancy for the sinking cases. Hollow shapes and trapped air are outside this solid-sphere comparison.

Precautions and limitations

Compare equal volumes and release conditions. These are chosen material-density examples, not measurements of particular household objects. Hollow shapes, trapped air and different volumes can change an object's average density and outcome. A vertical one-body numerical model uses partial-sphere buoyancy and illustrative quadratic water drag (coefficient 0.8), with a 1 ms semi-implicit integration step. Air drag, capillarity, added mass, fluid waves, rotation and rebound are excluded. Water level is prescribed and the tank is treated as broad. Bottom contact is perfectly inelastic. Force arrows are schematic; numerical readings are authoritative. Do not equate a transient frame with static equilibrium.
